# A Brief History of AI — and Why the Last Five Years Changed Everything

Artificial intelligence is not new. What is new is that, for the first time, general-purpose AI capability can be bought as a utility, integrated in weeks, and applied to knowledge work across every business function. Understanding how we got here helps organizations separate durable shifts from hype — and calibrate where they should invest.

## Seven Decades in Four Eras

| Era | Period | Defining idea | Business impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symbolic AI & expert systems | 1950s–1990s | Hand-coded rules and logic | Narrow, brittle, expensive to maintain |
| Statistical machine learning | 1990s–2012 | Learning patterns from data | Fraud detection, recommendations, forecasting — one model per problem |
| Deep learning | 2012–2020 | Neural networks + big data + GPUs | Vision, speech, translation reach production quality |
| Foundation models | 2020–today | One large pre-trained model, adapted to many tasks | General-purpose language, reasoning, and coding capability as a service |

Each era required progressively **less custom effort per use case**. That trend is the single most important thing to understand: the marginal cost of applying AI to a new problem has collapsed.

## The Long Winters — and Why This Time Is Different

AI has been through repeated cycles of promise and disappointment ("AI winters" in the 1970s and late 1980s). Skepticism is healthy. But three things distinguish the current wave:

- **Economic proof at scale.** Foundation models generate measurable productivity gains in coding, writing, support, and analysis — documented across thousands of enterprises, not just labs.
- **Distribution.** AI arrives through tools people already use: chat interfaces, IDEs, office suites, CRMs. Adoption friction is historically low.
- **A capability curve that keeps compounding.** Model capability, context length, and cost-efficiency have improved every year since 2020, with no plateau yet in sight.

## 2017–2020: The Quiet Setup

The groundwork for today's AI was laid before most executives noticed:

- **2017 — The Transformer.** Google's "Attention Is All You Need" paper introduced the architecture behind every modern large language model (LLM).
- **2018–2019 — Transfer learning works for language.** BERT and GPT-2 showed that models pre-trained on internet-scale text could be adapted to many tasks.
- **2020 — GPT-3 and scaling laws.** Capability was shown to grow predictably with model size, data, and compute. "Bigger is better" became an engineering roadmap, not a hope.

At this point AI was still a specialist's tool. That changed abruptly.

## 2021–2022: AI Learns to Code and Talks to Everyone

- **2021 — AI-assisted coding goes mainstream.** GitHub Copilot brought code generation into the developer's editor. Software engineering became AI's first deeply penetrated profession.
- **2022 — Image generation explodes.** DALL·E 2, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion made "type a sentence, get an image" real — the first mass-market taste of generative AI.
- **November 2022 — ChatGPT.** A conversational interface on top of an LLM reached 100 million users in two months, the fastest consumer product adoption in history. Overnight, every board asked: *"What is our AI strategy?"*

## 2023: The Enterprise Wakes Up

- **GPT-4 and Claude** demonstrated professional-exam-level reasoning, long-document comprehension, and reliable instruction following.
- **The API economy formed.** Enterprises could rent frontier capability per token — no data science team, no GPU cluster, no model training.
- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** became the standard pattern for grounding models in a company's own documents, reducing hallucination and unlocking internal knowledge.
- **Open-weight models** (Llama 2 and successors) gave organizations a self-hosted option for privacy-sensitive workloads.
- **Governance arrived**: the EU AI Act advanced, and enterprises stood up AI usage policies for the first time.

## 2024: Multimodal, Cheaper, and Grounded

- **Multimodality became normal.** Frontier models could see images, process documents, and increasingly hear and speak — one model handling text, vision, and structured data.
- **Cost collapsed.** Capability that cost dollars per task in 2022 fell to cents; smaller models (e.g. Haiku-class) made high-volume automation economical.
- **Context windows grew** to hundreds of thousands of tokens — entire codebases, contracts, or report archives in a single prompt.
- **Structured tool use matured.** Models learned to reliably call functions, query databases, and operate software — the technical foundation of agents.

## 2025–2026: The Agentic Era

The current frontier is AI that **does work**, not just answers questions:

- **Agents.** Models that plan multi-step tasks, use tools, browse, write and execute code, and check their own work. Agentic coding assistants (such as Claude Code) autonomously implement features, fix bugs, and open pull requests.
- **Reasoning models.** Models that "think before answering" dramatically improved performance on math, science, planning, and complex analysis.
- **Computer use.** Models can operate ordinary software through the screen, keyboard, and mouse — extending automation to systems without APIs.
- **Protocols and interoperability.** Standards like the Model Context Protocol (MCP) let one assistant securely reach many enterprise systems.
- **From copilots to coworkers.** The unit of adoption is shifting from "a feature in a tool" to "a digital team member with scoped responsibilities."

## What the Last Five Years Actually Enabled

Strip away the hype, and organizations gained five genuinely new capabilities:

1. **Language as an interface.** Any employee can direct software, query data, and generate work products in plain language — no specialist gatekeeper.
2. **Knowledge work automation.** Drafting, summarizing, translating, extracting, classifying, reviewing — the connective tissue of white-collar work — can now be delegated.
3. **Institutional memory on demand.** RAG and long context turn scattered documents, tickets, and wikis into an instantly queryable knowledge base.
4. **Software development at a new clock speed.** AI-assisted and agentic coding compresses build cycles, lowering the cost of internal tools and experimentation.
5. **Affordable intelligence at volume.** Tasks that were never worth a human's time (triaging every email, summarizing every call, checking every invoice) are now economical to do for *every* instance.

## Implications for Your Organization

- **The barrier is no longer technology — it's adoption.** Frontier capability is available to a 10-person firm on the same day it reaches a Fortune 500. Competitive advantage comes from data readiness, workflow redesign, and people, not model access.
- **Waiting has a cost.** Because capability compounds yearly, organizations that build AI fluency now will absorb each new wave (agents, computer use, whatever comes next) faster than those starting cold.
- **But sequencing matters.** The graveyard of failed AI projects is full of companies that skipped foundations — data quality, governance, change management — to chase demos. The rest of this material exists to get the sequence right.
